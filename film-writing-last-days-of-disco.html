Whit Stillman

The Last Days of Disco (1998)

This film is similar to its predecessors, in the way it covers a different era of this affluent American class, whether they’re UHPs in Metropolitan, Facha Americanas in Barcelona, or scum yuppies in The Last Days of Disco. In other class consciousness films, this group is usually scorned by the director and the audience, yet Stillman is able to bring out their naivety in such an endearing way. When Jimmy, Des, Charlotte, and the rest of the gang get kicked out of the club, they’re so baffled as to how they could be considered yuppies when they don’t meet x, y, z qualifications. Des makes this really meta point, “it’s usually the other people who get referred to as yuppie.”

What I like about Stillman’s films is that he likes to play with ideology— where do they stem from, and how to challenge the authenticity of his characters’ beliefs. More than his previous films, you can see how characters are painstakingly and hastily clutching onto a belief because they’re young, insecure, and need to distinguish themselves from the crowd. It’s funny because, to refer back to the yuppie quote earlier, they all stem from the same branch. What’s also great about Stillman’s style is that through his meticulously-written dialogue, which may be masked superficially as funny banter, the audience may get to understand how these characters are walking contradictions or have a flimsy sense of self. But Stillman isn’t patronizing about it at all.

The morally dubious characters like Charlotte and Des, who seem to have the strongest and loudest assertions, also flail when what they have verbalized has been questioned. Even separately, they’ll make a claim, and then if the character pushes them a little bit, they admit their actual thoughts. It’s really fascinating. Or there are characters like Dan, the coworker, who is incredibly judgmental about the club scene and the affluent lifestyles of the two women, when he’s one of those rich Ivy League students that cosplay as left-leaning communists and have warped beliefs about women. Tom, Alice’s first one-night stand, is incredibly threatened by Alice’s sexual empowerment and conjures up all these ideas about what a “slut” she is, only to realize that she had been a virgin the entire time; he also gave her two STDs.

What I also love about this film is that despite its realistic sets and camerawork, there’s this more metaphorical world-building. As I have mentioned earlier, Stillman’s films are about ideology, even if they don’t read as sociopolitical or on-the-nose with their messaging. The film is centered at the hottest disco club in New York, during the peak of disco. These characters are flirting, conversing, and falling in love, yet their innocence is about to be marred by the imminent demise of the club. This club feels like a micro-institution because it has its own customs, enforcement of those very customs, and ability to shape club goers’ thoughts and beliefs. The club promotes toxic relationships between men and women, so that it can continue socialization at the cost of authentic relationships. There are also strange customs that come off predatory, like the club owner’s arbitrary dislike of ad men. Additionally, the club hoards a lot of money and denies many people entering. I do want to make it clear, that when watching the film, it doesn’t come off like those painfully obvious allegories. Overall, there’s this idea of the disco club as an institution, and how it influences the socialization between the club goers and continues promoting its longevity on the block. The secret operation by law enforcement is this political resistance that transpires in this new era. The Last Days of Disco serves as the perfect end to this trilogy, because it has a more structural perspective on the fall of the dominant institution that shields this group of people in their innocence, and the scattered aftermath.
