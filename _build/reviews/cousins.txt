It’s interesting watching Le Beau Serge and Les Cousins back to back because with the first film, I thought Chabrol was sooo elitist. Now I see that Chabrol is able to poke fun at any class as long as they’re decadent and absentminded.

Compared to the long takes of the empty, hollow streets in Le Beau Serge, Paris is denser and more frenetic. This imagery of the big city eating up this country hick was seamlessly relayed by Henri Decaë’s camerawork. In one of the earlier scenes, when Paul receives a call about his girlfriend being pregnant, there’s this wonderful shot where Clovis and Paul are close up to the camera, occupying opposite ends of the frame, whereas Charles is in the middle of the frame, but far away. He’s approaching them. The audience is able to get that sense of these two men will domineer the social interactions with Charles, who just doesn’t know any better. Also, when Paul drives Charles around Paris, the shots switch between the city, the front of the car, and the back of their heads, causing a dizzying effect for the audience. Additionally, the soundtrack that disharmoniously combines orchestral music and banging sounds adds to the frenzy. These are examples of this implicit upper hand that Paul has over Charles, just because this is his stomping ground.

Chabrol neatly lays out the background characters that fill this rich, decadent world of university students. When Charles goes to the local bar with Paul, there are these pan shots that provide an overview of the cohort- we see a girl crying over Paul, Françoise and a guy making out, to Philippe’s detriment, guys playing cards, etc. No one is intellectual like Charles and reads Balzac, or is sensitive like him. When he goes to the bookshop next door, the owner felt like he met a rare breed, as the youth only like crime detective novels and other low brow content. These scenes capture how underneath the booze, the dancing, and the fun, this Parisian society is really apathetic and cold. An Italian minister starts shooting blanks during a party, and the crowd is completely blasé. Or when Philippe’s s*icide attempt is just a passing conversation. Nothing of substance is consequential because all of this can be paid for with money. It was funny when Florence and Paul were showering together, and she suddenly asked what the word sycophant meant, and neither of them knew.

Yet there’s this delicate balance of not making Charles a victim. He’s been so smug the moment that he arrived in Paris and thought he was better than the rest of Paul’s friends. It was hilarious when he labored away for his exam and failed, yet Paul just cheated and passed his. Even though Paul was a jerk for stealing Florence from Charles, it’s hard to give any sympathy to Charles. Just when I wondered whether Paul would get away with all his antics, Charles unwittingly handed this one on a plate for him, with full consequence.
