French New Wave · Jean-Luc Godard

Vivre Sa Vie (1962)

Godard distances the audience from the character and the plot by having time lapses, unclear narrative flow, and constrained cinematography. Yet, Anna Karina’s close-up expressions were extremely poignant, clueing us to feeling stifled yet wanting to take responsibility of her own life.

The cinematography was astute in the way that it demonstrated power struggle. Whenever Nana spoke with another man, being her ex-husband, Raoul, or the photographer, the camera would capture at least one of the participating character’s back heads. Even in the midst of vulnerable conversations, we couldn’t see either of their faces. When Nana is speaking with the photographer and he’s coaxing her to take an explicit photo, which she’s uncomfortable with, the camera focuses on Nana then the photographer, going back and forth. This movement turns the conversation into a small tiff, with each of them trying to steer it in their favor. There’s also a brilliant scene of Raoul and Nana at the cafe, and the frame barely squeezes in both their heads, while Raoul asks her to smile. She begrudgingly does so, and the camera aggressively shifts to fit her in the frame again. This was such a disturbing scene because she’s clearly uncomfortable, and I felt like Raoul was being so violent with his words, even if that wasn’t the surface-level intent.

The conversation with the philosopher was one of the rare moments that we see Nana express herself fully, and we find her arguing against his main point: that to speak is to think and one learns to speak well when they detach from life. For her, even if she isn’t verbally expressive, her stares express a deep emotional undercurrent. She doesn’t feel the need to speak or the need to become ambivalent to life. While the philosopher retorts, she turns to the camera and explicitly breaks the fourth wall. I felt like this was so bold because so many times in the film her expressions were demure or hidden, and this felt like a meta-like reaction to the film’s repeated attempts to stifle her in this constraining frame.

Finally, Godard uses intertextual references, like the Joan of Arc scene or the reading of Poe’s “The Oval Portrait”, to let the audience know that Nana is merely a character who will be sacrificed for the sake of art. Even though we don’t know much about her from the minimal details and inconsistent narrative of the film, we still heavily identify with her. I’m mentioning this because while Nana watches the Joan of Arc, the shot between the shot of Joan and the shot of Nana crying feels like this transference. Almost like visually, the film is communicating this responsibility of being a martyr, in this case of art, to Nana. Also, at the last vignette, her lover reads the excerpt of Poe’s “The Oval Portrait,” which is about a painter who obsessively paints his wife and ends up killing her. It’s really sad because Nana displays a lot of vulnerability with minimal expressions and I know she thinks she has control of her life. Yet, she’s a victim to art, and her life isn’t hers to live.
