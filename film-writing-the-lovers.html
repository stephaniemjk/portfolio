French New Wave · Louis Malle

The Lovers (1958)

In this film, I was really impressed by the visuals - the costumes, the camera shots, the lighting, and the setting to convey its message about the need for Jeanne to constantly perform her womanly duties and her inability to connect with herself.

Jeanne’s home in Dijon is really restraining. It echoes the French Neo-classical style, and the walls are covered in dark wood and the furniture is a bit dated and ornate. When her cool Paris friend Maggy visits her home, she comments how it’s old-fashioned and the latest trend is the Louis XV antique furniture. Her bedroom, which she doesn’t share with her husband, comes off more juvenile and contrasts with the darker, ornate living space, everything in her room is brighter and white. Even though Jeanne escapes her boring household duties in Paris, there’s this restraint there. We find her in more lively, public spaces, like dancing, the polo match, or the carnival, and she still carries this restraint and self-consciousness only the audience is privy to.

As Jeanne is relaying the latest hair trends from her visit to Paris, her husband is sat diagonally across from her. He nods along, but blows smoke in her direction, and that camera shot was so good. It immediately signals a rift between them- she’s consumed by the ritz and glamour while he finds that futile. The way Decaë captures Henri with his camerawork creates this visible, uncomfortable rift between him and his wife and her Paris life. They have an argument in her bedroom over her trip to Paris, and she’s dressed in all white with her laced canopy hanging above her bed. He’s wearing all black and hovers over her bed, with this dark shadow hanging behind him. The imagery really suggested that he was some kind of fairytale monster. I know I keep going on and on about the camerawork, but I loved the wide-angle frames of their dinner party with the couple and Jeanne’s Paris friends. It felt like an oil painting because there were simultaneous things happening at once. The setting is the library, and Raoul, Maggy, and Jeanne are bored and talking. Henri was in the back of the library, and he creeps up to them in the front. Henri constantly feels like an intruder to their secret life in Paris.

When Jeanne and Bernard roam around the forest behind her house, I was surprised they had that much space in their property. They were going on an adventure and the shots were beautiful. They were both wearing white, which Henri or Raoul had not worn, and as they were walking through the fields or the trees were hanging over them, this felt like a Romanticism painting. She finally felt liberated romantically and sexually with Bernard. However, going back to the trope, they both share an implicit understanding that their first night together was fleeting and cannot be replicated again.

Everyone and everything constrains Jeanne, both her Dijon and Paris life. Jeanne narrates over her actions in third person, like she’s disconnected from her real thoughts and desires. When she’s trying to reflect, we find her looking in the mirror- she’s either being vain or trying to gauge a reaction from herself. I’m sure other female characters like Maggy share that similar behavior, except they’re better at complying to it than Jeanne. At the end diner scene, she fully occupies the mirror that she briefly shared with Bernard; just like the beautiful nighttime Romanticism imagery that we don’t see again, the potential with that love was merely temporary. It seems like as a woman her way to escape her problems is to find another man. Sadly, the audience can only anticipate that she’ll perpetuate this cycle.
