French New Wave · Jean-Luc Godard

Masculin Féminin (1966)

The crux of this film is about the mismatched romance between Paul and Madeleine. He’s a communist, politically-minded, and yearns for classical music, while Madeleine is a pop singer and is dazzled by pop music and consumerism. A defining scene that indicates this incompatibility is when they’re watching a movie and Paul, out loud, complains about the new aspect ratio that is disrespectful to the filmmaker’s original intent. Madeleine does not care. He marches over to the projectionist, who is situated outside of the theater in a separate room, reads a passage out of the ISO standards, and vandalizes a unit outside. This scene also demonstrates how Paul doesn’t abide by the strict boundaries of public space. He talks out loud at the cinema, he vandalizes at will, or he intrudes Madeleine’s sound booth while she’s singing.

In Scene 3, Madeleine and Paul are trapped in a small bathroom and we see a series of back-and-forth close-ups. Close-ups are supposed to show how emotionally vulnerable they are, but they look away from the camera. But it ends up being a repetitive conversation because they won’t admit their true thoughts. Even when they have sex, the camera is positioned at the window from the outside. It’s not a beautiful love story about the French youth during the 60s but more about a generation that seems to intellectualize their feelings or deflect them, focusing on the shiny- whether they hide in their political activism or the shiny American toy.

Men all around Paul are being martyred for a cause, and we don’t even get to witness his reaction to them. A black man defends the sanitized interpretation of Bessie Smith’s music and a woman shoots him. A man, off camera, burns himself alive for the Vietnam War, in the same way that Buddhist Monk did against the RVN regime. From seeing these scenes, we see every action rendered as super politicized. Interestingly, Paul is the sole spectator for all of these tragedies and we never get to observe his emotional reactions. The scene cuts to the next, so abruptly, as if there’s something more interesting happening than this tragedy. These set of events represent how the film is really detached to the viewer.

I was really impressed with the editing because there’s a lot cooler techniques to mess with the meaning of sound and image and how a narrative is presented. From the English title, we know that there are 15 scenes, yet Godard plays with us by inconsistently adding the numbered title cards, often skipping some. It was funny because he added 4a for jokes. He would also obsessively start and/or end a scene with a door opening, and I tried counting and lost track. Instead of a clear transition, he would use a physical demarcation like that. I’m saying how he switches the meaning of what we expect to be scene transitions (title cards) with the diegetic part of the environment (the doors). There would be text intercutting scenes, without any relevance to the actual scene itself, and the current scene would resume. I’ve interpreted those title cards, such as mentioning the children of Marx and Coca-Cola. So if those title cards are diegetic, I believe they’re Paul’s thoughts. But besides intermittent narrations of events, we never get to see the characters be emotional or vulnerable. In the second to last scene, Paul surrenders his point-of-view in the story by admitting his objectivity while interviewing hasn’t been in good faith and decides to proceed against that; however, while he may think he’s being philosophically open-minded, that loss of objectivity cost him his life.

Overall, I really enjoyed this film because Godard gradually improves over time in reminding the audience of the artificiality of cinema. He really makes the audience question if what they’re seeing and hearing in terms of text, sound, and images all align and who they’re coming from. In creating this emotionally distant and perplexing environment, he introduces a spectrum of different social and political characters that are ultimately disinterested and overstimulated by the news, the shopping, and the city.
