I really enjoyed Elevator to the Gallows because it feels like a deconstructed film noir and it traverses different genres.

There was so much comedy because one thing set off a chain of events. Had the secretary not rung Tavernier’s phone, he would’ve remembered to take off the hook and the rope, which means that he wouldn’t have gotten stuck in the elevator, which means that the two teenagers wouldn’t have stolen his car transpiring in the second murder. I was also laughing when the little girl picked up the hook and the rope from the ground. This absurdity reminded me of Hitchcock’s films, where one mindless mistake bubbles into a huge disaster, and Hitchcock and Malle both play God in the narrative.

I was also impressed by the three storylines and the different genres they encompassed. Even though the overarching genre is noir, Tavernier’s experience in the elevator feels like a psychological thriller, those trapped room horrors. Florence’s storyline feels like a melodrama with all the close-ups, internal monologues, and longing after her lover; actually, she’s the only character who externalizes her thoughts. The two teenagers’ storyline feels like a road movie, reacting against car culture and the infrastructure developed around long-distance driving. The motel they stayed at looked incredibly Tati-esque, reminiscent of Mon Oncle. Going back to my main point about the subversion of noir, it’s funny that these characters’ nighttimes and the lead up take up 2/3 of the film, and the discovery of the crime and the law enforcement’s work happened so quickly and swiftly. Usually, in noir films, the detective is trying to solve for clues.

It’s so ironic that in classic noir films, the evils or nuttiness of men drive the plot, but in this case technology is such an obstruction for these characters. To be specific, in the way these characters are a little confused about these appliances. As someone from the digital age and who has consumed tons of true crime podcasts, we would never keep photos of us with our appliances foolishly in our camera. Or the way that Tavernier forgot the elevator would be shut down after work hours, and he tried thinking he could play around with the engineering. This reminded me of some man v. technology trope. The perpetrators don’t really understand these devices so they give themselves away to law enforcement pretty quickly, allowing the crime-solving to resolve itself quicker.

The two murders of Carala and the Germans made me ruminate on how masculinity was changing and was fraught and generational differences. This was post-WWII France and the wars in Vietnam and Algeria were no longer cool and celebrated, and there was this cultural, political shift that the youth represents. Yet there’s this competing force of the influx of consumerism. Louis and Véronique observe Tavernier from afar. While Louis snickers at Tavernier who is a veteran and corporate man, he actually feels really insecure. He feels similarly to the German older man who jabs at him for lying about his identity and the fact that he served in the military. When Louis was caught attempting to hijack the car, he mistook the German man’s cigar tube for a gun— that’s how insecure he is.

Similarly, Tavernier’s relationship to his boss, Carala, is in the similar vein because he profited off the wars that Tavernier, a younger man, fought endlessly in. Tavernier says to Carala that “[war] is his family heirloom.” While Carala’s murder was primarily motivated by wanting to be with Florence, there was an ancillary reason for avenging the wheel that inflicted so much pain to him and other soldiers.

While the surface level plot is about two lovers wanting to be together, it’s a greater portrait of changing expectations of masculinity, generational divides about social and political issues, the emergence of technology, the American export of car and suburban culture, and so on. All executed in this screwball way of trying to be criminals.
