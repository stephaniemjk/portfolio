Whit Stillman is able to use dialogue to demarcate time rather than using obvious, visual settings. Also through dialogue, the film demonstrates how ideologies clash amongst Ted, Fred, and Ramon. The audience is introduced to a little-known trade show in Barcelona. In his films, Stillman has a pattern of strongly establishing a cultural norm that doesn’t make intuitive, immediate sense to the audience, and we have to roll with it— similar to Sally Fowler’s superior Rat Pack or the disco night club.

In the film’s dialogues, it makes use of semiotics by offering clear distinction between what was said and the interpretation, or the intended one, of it. Ted dutifully abides by the written text of Dale Carnegie and other business/self-help and quotes them quite often, but it’s to reinforce this image of himself as moral and strategic. For example, Ted quotes this sales book about Maneuver X and how he’ll use that technique to salvage his relationship with Aurora. Fred reduces the fluff of what he said to a simple idea: he’s playing hard-to-get. Ted is offended by the simplistic interpretation, and reiterates that Maneuver X is different. Comically, Ted will quote these phrases from these self-help business books, and they’re common-sense tips about being thoughtful and diligent. Yet he’s wowed by them, and this willing abidance shows his impressionability and how he needs to cling onto the branding interpreted by these books than the actual content themselves. Fred is the most interesting character because he’s able to manipulate language in some semiotics sense. He frequently takes things from Ted without letting him know beforehand, and Ted rightfully gets upset. When Ted interprets his behavior as theft, Fred is offended and reframes it as taking it before letting him know or merely borrowing. This kind of malleability with language represents the Pax Americanism that quickly moved across the globe. It’s not that America is invading other countries or fueling proxy wars; it’s merely “defending democracy” against the imminent communist threat. Fred’s character perfectly splits between actions and words, and it demonstrates while his language might warrant action, his actions create negative repercussions. However, Stillman isn’t someone to come off pro- or anti-American, and he lets the character of Fred remain innocent and endearing, just to clarify that his behavior isn’t intentionally malicious.

The trade show stokes the insecure feelings of masculinity and sexuality in all three characters, Ted, Fred, and Ramon. Despite the cultural barriers, the ultimate goal of a man is to find a woman. There are very obvious differences between the two cousins and Ramon. Ramon represents probably the Catalan and anti-American resistance during the period of Spain’s entry into NATO. He clashes with the two cousins in terms of masculinity, as he wields the power and influence over the trade women, gatekeeping them to the two men, and he also is polyamorous. Meanwhile, Ted and Fred represent American values with the goal of a nuclear family by their beautiful lake house and believing in America’s hegemony as more benevolent than not. Despite their unified resistance to the hostile natives, they clash with one another. In the first act of the film, as Aurora and Ted are lying down on bed, she talks about Ramon’s conviction: that God is dead from the old world and beauty is the new divinity. The rejection of God had continued to propagate into the 20th century, and with this uncertainty of this one god, Ted, Fred, and Ramon embody different ideologies to ascertain divinity. In my early paragraph, I talked about the use of dialogue to reinforce ideology, and I’ll explicate that sentence. Ted’s the classic of the triad, as he’s Protestant, believes in chastity, and has this religious dedication to corporate institution. He embodies that Protestant belief of hard work and morality leads to a fruitful life. Underneath that facade, he’s really insecure about the authenticity of attractive women and talks about abstinence, but immediately caves when he meets Aurora. Second, Fred is not religious but has a devotion to serving one’s country. Meanwhile, Fred is a Navy officer who keeps wearing his military uniform around (quite topically) when even his boss told him to stop. However, there’s this revealing scene. After Fred fixes the xenophobia graffiti with his sharpie pen, an attractive woman walks up and asks what he’s doing, and he hides his previous action. Finally, Ramon is the naughtier one of the triad. He is direct and believes in the divinity of beauty and women, and his perspective opposes the American dream of a nuclear family. In Barcelona, there’s more chaos and women have finally embraced sexual liberation much later than women in other western countries. In Barcelona, both men and women embrace polyamory and the relational ambiguity, Ramon is able to benefit from this confusion. Additionally, because of his role as a journalist, he’s able to disseminate information like when he wrote about Fred being part of the CIA. All of their ideologies are underpinned by the same root cause; they’re competing with one another to be desirable. Both their goals contradict with one another, one embracing polyamory and the other embracing sexual liberation. This is some allegory for the very fertile soil of Spain right after Franco’s rule, where it was leaving the fascist regime, and there was this effort to claim Spain.

Because his films are concealed by the funny and witty dialogue and very wealthy people, the understanding of his films is pretty limited, when his films delve much deeper into how knowledge is created. The scenes are much shorter, and there’s a black-out that follows a big revelation or a quippy sentence. We find out that Aurora had a partner in the first act of the film, and we don’t get to see Ted’s reaction. Or when that picnic with Ramon, Ted makes an analogy of America’s foreign policy and reduces the colonies to ants. He begins blabbering out of nervousness, and Fred makes the atmosphere worse by throwing a rock at the ants. It’s interesting that we never get to see the emotional aftermath of these tense scenes, and we only see the characters having processed what happened. This technique reinforces the sardonic, emotionally distant nature of the characters, and it removes the audience from being able to identify with the characters. Perhaps, the characters are depicted as tropes and the audience is not meant to humanize and understand them.

This primal reason that motivates their ideologies, to find a woman, reminds me of Dr. Strangelove, in which the military higher ups and cabinet members were hilariously launching this devastating nuclear catastrophe all because of their inability to reconcile with their sexuality or their horniness. At the end of the film, Ted, Fred, and Dickie are able to bring over the three trade show women to America, almost in this post-Vietnam-war-military-wives kind of way. There’s this sanguine imagery of this supposed cultural harmony by the men flipping the hamburger on the grill and telling the women, “it’s not that bad,” as they giggle. What I like about Whit Stillman’s films is that he doesn’t shy away from covering this upper class of America, but he doesn’t resort to heavy-handed class-conscious, sociopolitical messaging. He finds the characters endearing, but isn’t afraid to challenge the root of their causes, and there’s no dramatic, devastating ending.
