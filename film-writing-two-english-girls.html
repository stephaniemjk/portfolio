French New Wave · François Truffaut

Two English Girls (1971)

I think Two English Girls and The 400 Blows are my favorite Truffaut films by far. It’s a continuation of the same complicated romance and societal themes explored in Jules and Jim, except this version feels more mature. They’re both based on the novels by Henri-Pierre Roche, who seems to have a penchant for depicting complicated love triangles. While Jules and Jim explored the ideas of literary adaptations during nouvelle vague and strongly wove in intertextual references from other film eras, I wasn’t as emotionally invested in it as I was with Two English Girls. With this film, Truffaut demonstrates more maturity, emotional depth, and continues to make references to other film and art movements.

As much as I appreciate Raoul Coutard’s previous collaboration with Truffaut, I think utilizing a new cinematographer, Nestor Almendros was fantastic. He was a frequent collaborator of Eric Rohmer’s Six Moral Tales series, and like in all of his works, he employs color well to externalize the character’s emotional state onto the background and they’re truthfully just pretty to look at. The underlying message about the film is about the Belle Epoque period, when France was culturally and economically at its peak, and there was collaboration between the English and the French for the first time, but France possessed the upper hand in that dynamic. The England we saw was this beautiful countryside that was reminiscent of older Landscape paintings, and the glitzy Paris we saw was the denser Parisian society paintings of Renoir. There was this parochial and naive association with the two sisters’ English backgrounds compared to the worldly and depraved behavior of Claude.

Almendros’ frames were so stunning. This film felt like an homage to paintings and portraiture. There’s this one tracking shot filmed on a canoe of looking at Claude and Ann on that rustic house from afar. We track her walking across the lake house property, and the leaves cloud us from the full image. It felt very impressionistic. The entirety of this film felt like an ode to impressionism and romantic landscape paintings. When I see these society paintings by Renoir, I often wonder what they’re talking about, what they’re thinking about. The characters are never that expressive themselves, and the only scene that was super vulnerable was the final meeting between Muriel and Claude. Yet the way the film was styled compensated for the lack of honesty from the characters. The beautiful settings, with the colored walls and furniture, were deliberative and reminded me of the visuals in Hitchcock’s Vertigo. The film challenges how color is used to connect with the characters’ interiority.

There’s this metaphor they occasionally referred to this threesome relationship of Claude and the two sisters- the waterfall. Ann is the lip of the waterfall and Muriel is basin; they both start and end the waterfall. Claude occupies a more dynamic role as the falling water. The fluidity of the water can’t guarantee permanence in their relationship and makes their relationship cyclical as well. This opposes with Claude’s mother’s use of metaphor- hers is more grounded, saying that she built Claude’s foundation using bricks.

Similar to the idea of eras changing in Jules and Jim, the film looks at it more narrowly and the in-between Romanticism and Belle Epoque. Around this time, the idea of the new woman - this educated and modern woman - became salient. All of this is to talk about how the imagery and Ideas were pretty definitive in that Claude could never pursue a real relationship with these women. Additionally, Ann’s role was always sacrificial and her romance with Claude would lead to Muriel, never the other way around. There’s some fire, water, wind, and earth imagery where earth and water can appreciate each other from afar, but never be connected.
